Resource Deprivation and How It Affects Us

Introduction

Resource Deprivation in Agriculture and Fishery Arts

Resource deprivation is a crisis that strips people of the essentials needed to live, work, and thrive: food, clean water, healthcare, education, housing, and stable income. It does not strike evenly. In the Philippines, overfishing and poor waste management have historically driven food insecurity, malnutrition, poverty, and debt, and today they manifest as the displacement of fisherfolk and worsening floods. Across other countries, structural inequality and rural asset scarcity have marginalized the poor and limited long-term development. And within communities today, high input costs and heavy import tariffs have reduced crop and fish production, deepened dependence on middlemen, and left local ecosystems unable to sustain their populations.

This research examines resource deprivation in agriculture and fishery arts across three levels — people in the Philippines, people in other countries, and communities today — tracing each through its root causes, historical impact during the Green and Blue Revolution eras, and present-day consequences. From the collapse of fish populations to the indebtedness of farmers, the evidence shows that deprivation is not an accident but a cycle: one that traps families in poverty, forces migration, and deepens inequality. Yet it also points toward a path forward — one where resources are shared fairly, made affordable, and preserved so that both people and the ecosystems they depend on can endure.

About the Problem

A Tug of War Between Populations and Ecosystems

Resource deprivation is a tug of war between populations and ecosystems. When people cannot access the essentials of life — food, clean water, healthcare, education, housing, and employment — they are shut out. Hunger follows, poverty deepens, and the chance to build a better life slips away. Families lose their livelihoods, communities break, and inequality widens between those who have and those who have not.

The effects ripple outward. In communities, the loss of clean water, fertile soil, and timber triggers food and water insecurity, strips away livelihoods, and creates competition for what little remains. Poverty is worsened, families are forced to migrate, and social unrest follows. Meanwhile, the local ecosystems that once sustained these populations can no longer carry the weight. When the natural world is exhausted, so too are the people who depend on it — and the cycle of deprivation begins again.

Research Findings

What the Evidence Shows

In the Philippines, overfishing and poor waste management drive food insecurity and displacement. Internationally, structural inequality and rural resource scarcity cause marginalization and displacement. And among communities today, high input costs and tariffs reduce production and create dependence on the elite.

People in the Philippines

Poor Waste Management

Causes garbage to accumulate in drainage systems and waterways, worsening floods during heavy rainfall and typhoons.

Overfishing

Leads to the collapse of fish populations and habitat destruction.

People in Other Countries

Resource Deprivation in Other Countries

The root causes of resource deprivation are poverty, inequality, lack of resources, and limited access to modern tools and technology. These problems make it difficult for farmers and fishermen to earn enough money and provide food for their families.

Impact on Communities Today

Resource Deprivation in Communities Today

Resource deprivation in communities today is caused by poverty, unfair access to resources, and the high cost of farming and fishing supplies. Seeds, fertilizers, fuel, and equipment can be too expensive for many farmers and fishermen.

Impact

How Deprivation Shapes Lives

People in the Philippines

12.8%

Food insecurity has been a problem for centuries. Back then it led to loss of livelihoods, malnutrition, displacement, dependence on imports for basic nutrition, and poverty. The Green Revolution is also a big factor that pushed farmers into indebtedness, hunger, and poverty. And up until now, poverty still affects us: according to the Philippine Statistics Authority, 12.8% of Filipinos are multidimensionally poor.

People in Other Countries

Resource deprivation is the lack of access to basic needs such as food, water, healthcare, education, housing, and jobs. Its main root causes are poverty, inequality, and lack of resources. In farming and fishing, political problems, environmental damage, and limited resources make it harder for workers to earn a living. Historically, it has increased poverty and limited development, especially in rural and coastal communities.

Impact on Communities Today

Because of resource deprivation, farmers and fishermen may produce less and earn less. Small producers also struggle to compete with bigger businesses. As a result, families may have trouble affording food and meeting their basic needs.

Historical Impacts

The Green and Blue Revolutions

The Green and Blue Revolutions were intended to modernize farming and fishing. As described in this research, these changes also marginalized poorer producers, and the Green Revolution pushed some farmers into chemical debt, hunger, and poverty. Their effects help explain how past approaches to production can shape resource access and livelihoods today.

Lessons Learned

Four Takeaways From the Research

  • Deprivation is a cycle, not an accident. Overfishing and poor waste management don't just deplete resources — they trigger a chain reaction of food insecurity, poverty, debt, and displacement that repeats across generations.
  • Historical policies shape today's problems. The Green and Blue Revolutions were meant to modernize farming and fishing, but they pushed farmers into chemical debt and marginalized the poor.
  • The solution is resource equity. Sharing resources fairly based on community needs — not equal distribution — is what actually improves lives.
  • The problem is solvable, but it needs awareness first. The first step is understanding the cycle.

Did You Know?

Open each card to reveal a quick fact.

Water scarcity

Water scarcity can result from both limited supplies and difficulty accessing safe, reliable water. Protecting watersheds and reducing pollution can help communities.

Deforestation

Forests help protect soil, store water, and provide habitats. When forests are cleared, erosion and loss of wildlife habitat can increase.

Overfishing

When fish are caught faster than populations can recover, fish stocks can decline and fishing communities may lose an important source of food and income.

Pollution

Waste entering rivers and coastal waters can harm wildlife, affect public health, and damage resources that communities rely on.
